Clear Pricing

Clear Pricing Before Any Work Starts

Before we touch a tool, we find out exactly what's wrong. Our technicians look at the age and size of the tank, the burner or elements, the relief valve, the venting, and any signs of corrosion or seepage. After that you get an honest recommendation and the full price up front, whether the job is replacing a thermocouple or installing a new heater. No hidden fees, no surprise add-ons.

The trouble with water heater failures is that most of them start out of sight. Hard-water minerals settle into a crust on the tank floor, the anode rod wears down gradually, and when it's gone, the tank itself starts to corrode. When hot water comes out rusty, the tank rumbles, or a small drip appears at the base, have it checked before a repair turns into a replacement.

Not every visit is about a breakdown. Plenty of homeowners ask us to size up an aging heater before it fails, and it's a smart move. With a typical tank lifespan of 8 to 12 years (tankless units often run 20 years with annual descaling), a replacement on your own timeline beats a rushed swap after a leak. We'll give you an honest read on how much life your equipment has left.

Annual Flushing

Water Heater Maintenance That Adds Years

Routine maintenance is the most reliable way to keep hot water flowing year-round. Regular service prevents failures before they start, clears out sediment, and catches small faults while they're still inexpensive to fix. At Philadelphia Water Heater, every maintenance visit is built around your specific equipment, covering a full tank flush, an anode rod inspection, a test of the temperature and pressure relief valve, a burner and flue check on gas units, element and thermostat testing on electric units, and a thermostat setting check.

Many homes do best with a yearly flush, and houses on hard water often need a visit every six months. The schedule flexes with the age of your heater, how hard your household runs it, and the mineral content of your water, which determines how fast sediment accumulates and how long an anode rod lasts.

The savings show up faster than most people expect. A flushed tank heats water with less effort, a replaced anode rod keeps corrosion off the tank walls, and a tested relief valve is ready to do its job if pressure ever climbs too high. Compared with the cost of replacing a tank early, a maintenance plan is money well spent.

Sized for Your Household

A Water Heater Sized for Your Actual Household

Bigger isn't always better when it comes to water heaters. What matters for a tank is the first-hour rating, how many gallons of hot water it can deliver in one busy hour, and for a tankless unit it's the GPM it can hold when the incoming water is cold. That's why we never just copy the size of the old heater. We look at how many people live in the home, the number of bathrooms, and big draws like a soaking tub or a high-capacity washer.

Choosing a new water heater comes down to more than tank size. Our technician will compare tankless units against a traditional tank, and gas against electric or hybrid, laying out operating costs, venting and gas line requirements, electrical capacity, and the installation itself. Tankless works well for many households, though it often requires a bigger gas line and new venting, while a well-sized tank is often the simpler, lower-cost option. The decision is yours, based on your home and budget.

Frequent Failures

The Water Heater Repairs Local Homes Need Most

Homes around Philadelphia run into a familiar set of water heater failures, and each one calls for a different fix. Philadelphia Water Heater handles them all: no hot water, water that only gets lukewarm, pilots that won't stay lit, electric tanks that trip the breaker, rumbling tanks full of sediment, rusty or smelly water, leaking relief valves, and tankless units flashing error codes. Our technicians identify the exact fault and target the repair to the component that actually failed.

The right repair depends entirely on what actually failed. On electric tanks, that usually means an element or thermostat. On gas units, it tends to be a thermocouple, flame sensor, or gas valve. Pressure problems get solved with an expansion tank plus a properly set pressure-reducing valve, and leaks at the drain valve or fittings get repaired before they spread. We also verify the thermostat setting, since water above 120°F can scald young children and older adults in seconds. A tank leaking from the steel itself can't be patched, and we'll tell you so. And if you ever smell gas near the heater, leave the house and call your gas utility right away.
